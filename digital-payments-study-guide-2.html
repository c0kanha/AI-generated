How a Card Payment Actually Works

The seven specialists, the two-second authorization round-trip, the slow-moving money behind it, and the security layers that keep the whole thing from being looted.

The Master Analogy: A Two-Lane Highway

The single most useful idea in payments is this: a "transaction" is actually two separate events disguised as one.

  • Lane 1 — the fast lane (authorization). A two-second round-trip conversation. A question — "Can this card spend this much?" — flies through a chain of intermediaries, reaches the cardholder's bank, and an answer comes back. No money moves. Just a hold, and a green check on the merchant's screen.
  • Lane 2 — the slow lane (settlement). Hours or days later, an armored money truck travels the same chain, and the actual funds shift from the cardholder's bank into the merchant's bank, minus fees taken by every party along the way.

Once you see the two lanes, every other piece of payments — gateways, processors, networks, tokenization, chargebacks, interchange fees — slots into one or the other. This guide walks you through the cast, both lanes, the security around them, and what changes when you step beyond cards.

01 The Cast — Seven Specialists, Not Three

Most explanations of card payments collapse the whole pipeline into "the bank" and "the website." There are actually seven distinct roles, and a lot of confusion disappears once you can name them.

RoleWhat they doExample
CardholderThe person paying.You.
MerchantThe business being paid.An online store.
Payment gatewaySoftware that captures card data on the merchant's site and forwards it securely.Stripe Elements, Braintree.js, Authorize.Net.
Payment processorTalks to the card network on behalf of the merchant; ferries auth and settlement messages.Worldpay, Fiserv, Adyen.
Acquiring bank (acquirer)Holds the merchant's account; receives funds during settlement.Chase Merchant Services, Wells Fargo Merchant.
Card network (scheme)Routes messages between acquirer and issuer; sets the rulebook and fees.Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover, JCB, UnionPay.
Issuing bank (issuer)Issued the card to the cardholder; ultimately approves or declines each charge.Chase, Citi, Bank of America, Capital One.

Two reasons this matters. First, every fee, every dispute, and every fraud rule is owned by a specific actor in this list — knowing who owns what tells you who to call. Second, in modern integrated services these roles are bundled: Stripe is gateway + processor + acquirer-of-record; American Express is network + issuer. The bundles are the exception, the seven roles are the rule.

✓ Why know all seven

  • Every fee on a merchant statement maps to one of these roles
  • Disputes (chargebacks) hop role-by-role through the chain
  • You can decode any payments diagram once you've named the actors

✗ Where it gets messy

  • Bundled providers (Stripe, PayPal, Adyen) hide the seams — convenient, but the roles still exist behind the API
  • Closed-loop networks (Amex, Discover) collapse the network and issuer roles into one company
  • "Processor" and "acquirer" are often the same legal entity in practice

02 Authorization — The Fast Lane

When you click "Buy," what happens in the next ~2 seconds is a question-and-answer round-trip. Card data leaves your browser and flies through the chain to your bank, which makes the actual yes/no decision. Every other actor is a relay or a checker — none of them can approve or decline a charge themselves.

Step by step:

  1. Your browser sends card data over TLS to the merchant's site, which immediately hands it to the gateway.
  2. The gateway validates format (Luhn check on the card number, expiration date, CVV present), then sends an authorization request to the processor.
  3. The processor routes the request through the card network to the issuing bank.
  4. The issuer evaluates: is the card real and active? Are there sufficient funds or credit? Does the AVS billing-address data match? Does the CVV match? Does the transaction look fraudulent?
  5. The issuer returns an auth code (approve) or a decline code (e.g., "51 — insufficient funds," "05 — do not honor"). The funds are placed on hold but not yet moved.
  6. The response travels back the same path: issuer → network → processor → gateway → merchant. The merchant's screen shows green.

✓ What this gets right

  • Single round-trip with a single point of approval — fast and simple to reason about
  • The issuer is the source of truth for every funds and fraud decision
  • Industry-standard ISO 8583 (and increasingly ISO 20022) message formats keep everyone speaking the same language

✗ What can go wrong

  • An "approved" auth doesn't guarantee funds — issuers can later reverse on suspected fraud
  • The hold expires (typically 7–30 days) if the merchant doesn't capture the funds
  • "Soft declines" (insufficient funds, do not honor) and "hard declines" (lost/stolen card) require very different retry strategies

03 Settlement — The Slow Lane Where Money Actually Moves

The auth code is just a promise. The actual transfer of funds happens later through a separate process called clearing and settlement. This is the lane most explanations skip — but it's where the money lives.

The merchant's POS or e-commerce system collects all the day's authorized transactions and submits a capture file (often at end-of-day) to the processor. The processor then aggregates these into a settlement batch sent through the network. The network performs clearing (matching auth records to capture records, exchanging the official transaction data between issuer and acquirer), then settlement happens: the issuer's bank wires funds to the acquiring bank, which credits the merchant's account — typically 1–3 business days after the original authorization.

Along the way, fees are deducted from the merchant's gross amount:

  • Interchange — paid by the acquirer to the issuer. The largest component, typically 1.5%–3.0% of the transaction. Set by the network but kept by the issuer.
  • Scheme/network fees — paid to Visa/Mastercard for using their rails. Small per-transaction fees.
  • Processor/acquirer markup — what the processor and acquiring bank charge for their services on top of interchange.

This is why the merchant nets less than the customer paid. A $100 card sale might land $97.10 in the merchant's account, three days later.

✓ Why batched settlement exists

  • Batching is dramatically cheaper than settling each transaction in real time
  • Gives merchants a window to issue refunds or void transactions before money moves
  • Allows clean daily reconciliation — one deposit per batch, easy to match in accounting

✗ Where it hurts

  • 1–3 day settlement creates real cash-flow strain for small merchants
  • Chargebacks can claw funds back weeks or months after settlement
  • Interchange is opaque and varies by card type — premium rewards cards cost merchants the most

04 Security Layers — Defense in Depth

Card data is a lucrative target, so the industry layers several defenses on top of each other. The right mental model isn't "encryption or tokenization" — it's all of these, simultaneously, mandated by PCI DSS.

LayerWhat it doesWhat it doesn't do
TLS Encrypts data on the wire between browser, merchant, and gateway. Reversible at each endpoint. Doesn't protect data once it's sitting in a database.
Encryption at rest Stored card data (if any) is encrypted with AES-256. Key management is the hard part. Useless if the keys are stolen alongside the data.
Tokenization Replaces the real PAN (card number) with a random surrogate ("token"). The real PAN lives in a hardened vault. The vault becomes the new high-value target — security shifts to the vault provider.
Network tokens Tokens issued by Visa/Mastercard themselves. Auto-update when the underlying card is reissued. Network-specific — different scheme = different token.
3-D Secure (3DS) An extra cardholder authentication step (e.g., one-time code) during checkout. Shifts fraud liability from merchant to issuer. Adds checkout friction and can hurt conversion.
PCI DSS The industry standard everyone touching card data must follow — covers networks, storage, access, monitoring. Compliance is not the same as security; it's a floor, not a ceiling.

Two ideas worth holding onto. Encryption protects data at every stage — in transit and at rest — by making it unreadable without a key. Tokenization takes a stronger stance: it removes the sensitive data from the merchant's environment entirely. The merchant stores a token; the token is worthless to a thief without access to the vault. Most modern stacks use both, plus 3DS for risky transactions.

Highway view: Picture each of the seven cities as a fortress. TLS is the sealed armored car between fortresses. Encryption-at-rest is the locked safe inside each fortress. Tokenization is a "claim ticket" system — the actual valuables stay in one super-fortress (the vault), and every other fortress holds only redemption tickets. 3DS is the bouncer at the customer's door who asks "are you really you?" before letting the trip start.
Draw in Excalidraw: A castle-and-moat scene. Center: a heavily fortified central tower labeled "Token Vault (the only place real PANs live)". Surrounding it, six smaller towers labeled Browser, Merchant, Gateway, Processor, Acquirer, Network. Between every tower, draw an arrow labeled "TLS". Inside each tower, a small safe labeled "AES-256 at rest". A dotted line from the merchant tower into the vault labeled "Token → Real PAN (only on demand)". A guard at the browser tower labeled "3DS check."

✓ Why defense in depth wins

  • An attacker has to defeat multiple, independent controls to extract anything useful
  • Tokenization shrinks PCI scope — fewer systems "touch" real card data, fewer audits
  • 3DS+EMV+tokenization together push fraud rates near zero on properly configured stacks

✗ Where defenses break

  • Compromised endpoint (malware on the merchant's server) can defeat TLS by reading data before encryption
  • Token vaults are catastrophic single points of failure if breached
  • 3DS friction can lower conversion rates 5–15% if applied indiscriminately

05 Beyond Cards — Wallets, Bank Rails, and Real-Time Payments

Cards aren't the only way to pay digitally — they're just the most familiar in the US. Around the world, several other rails (the underlying transfer system) carry a huge and growing share of payments. Knowing what's a "rail" versus what's a "wrapper" clears up most of the confusion.

Card rails (everything in chapters 1–4): Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover, JCB, UnionPay. Strengths: ubiquitous, instant authorization, strong consumer protections. Weaknesses: 1.5–3% merchant cost, 1–3 day settlement.

Bank rails move money directly between bank accounts, no card involved:

  • ACH (US) — cheap (~$0.25), slow (1–3 days), used for payroll, rent, recurring billing.
  • SEPA (Europe) — bank transfers across the eurozone; SEPA Instant settles in seconds.
  • UPI (India) — real-time, free for consumers, dominant for retail; Pix (Brazil) and PromptPay (Thailand) are similar.
  • FedNow (US, launched 2023) and RTP — instant bank-to-bank rails finally arriving in the US.
  • Faster Payments (UK) — instant bank transfers since 2008.

Wallets (Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal, Venmo, Cash App) are wrappers, not rails. They sit on top of an underlying card or bank account. Apple Pay, for example, replaces your card number with a network token and adds a biometric authentication step — but the actual money still moves on Visa or Mastercard rails underneath.

All-in-one PSPs (Stripe, Adyen, PayPal, Square, Block, Mollie) bundle gateway + processor + acquiring relationships across many of these rails. One API, one contract, one dashboard — at the cost of bundled (often higher) pricing.

Highway view: Cards are one specific highway with seven cities and well-known tolls. Bank rails are an entirely different highway system — fewer cities, lower tolls, sometimes faster. Wallets aren't roads at all; they're cars that drive on whichever highway is available. All-in-one PSPs sell you a single ticket that's valid on any of these roads.

✓ Why this matters now

  • Real-time bank rails are growing faster than card rails — UPI processes more transactions monthly than Visa globally
  • Bank rails cost a fraction of card fees, attractive for high-ticket B2B
  • Wallets give you tokenization + strong authentication "for free" on top of legacy rails

✗ Where the picture frays

  • Bank rails generally lack the chargeback protections cardholders take for granted
  • Wallets are platform-locked: Apple Pay is iOS-only; some networks aren't supported in some regions
  • "All-in-one" doesn't mean "best for everyone" — large merchants often save millions by negotiating direct acquirer relationships

Plain-English Recap — The Restaurant Version

Here is a simplified breakdown of the two vital systems that make digital payments possible.

To understand the difference, it helps to think of an online transaction like dining at a restaurant:

  • The Payment Gateway is the waiter. They take your order (card details), make sure it looks correct, and securely carry it to the kitchen.
  • The Payment Processor is the kitchen. They actually do the heavy lifting of preparing the meal (moving the money between the banks).

Payment Gateway: The Front-End Security Guard

A payment gateway is a software application that acts as the secure bridge between a merchant's website and the payment processor.

  • Primary Role: It captures the customer's payment details at checkout, encrypts them (so hackers can't read them), and sends them off for approval.
  • Customer-Facing: When you type your credit card number into a checkout page, you are interacting directly with the payment gateway.
  • Analogy: It acts as the "virtual" version of the physical card reader you dip your chip into at a physical store.

Payment Processor: The Back-End Money Mover

A payment processor is the financial entity operating behind the scenes that actually executes the transaction.

  • Primary Role: It receives the encrypted data from the gateway and contacts the card networks (Visa, Mastercard) and the customer's bank (the Issuing Bank) to ask a simple question: "Does this person have enough money/credit for this purchase?"
  • Bank-Facing: It manages the complex communication between the banks and ultimately facilitates the settlement (the actual transfer of funds from the buyer's account to the seller's account).

The Journey of a Digital Transaction

It's not just magic; it's a very fast committee meeting. Here is how they work together in milliseconds:

  1. Initiation: You click "Buy Now" on a website.
  2. The Gateway: The Payment Gateway encrypts your card data and safely transmits it to the Payment Processor.
  3. The Processor: The Payment Processor routes this request through the card network to your bank.
  4. The Bank: Your bank checks your balance, approves or declines the request, and sends the answer back to the Processor.
  5. The Relay: The Processor tells the Gateway the result. The Gateway displays "Payment Successful!" (or "Card Declined") on your screen.
  6. Settlement: A few days later, the Processor actually moves the funds into the merchant's bank account.

07 Payments Glossary — The Vocabulary

The terms you'll see in any payments documentation, dashboard, or merchant statement. Keep this open in another tab the first few times you read a settlement report.

TermDefinition
3DS / 3-D SecureCardholder authentication step (e.g., Visa Secure, Mastercard ID Check). Shifts fraud liability from merchant to issuer when used.
ACHAutomated Clearing House — US bank-to-bank rail. Cheap, slow (1–3 days), used for payroll, bills, recurring debits.
Acquirer / Acquiring BankThe merchant's bank — receives funds during settlement and assumes liability for the merchant's transactions.
Authorization (Auth)Issuer's approval to hold funds for a transaction. Doesn't move money on its own.
AVSAddress Verification Service — issuer compares the billing address provided at checkout to the one on file.
BINBank Identification Number — first 6–8 digits of a card number. Identifies the issuer, card type, country.
BNPLBuy Now, Pay Later — installment credit at checkout (Klarna, Affirm, Afterpay).
CaptureStep that converts an authorization hold into a request for actual settlement. Often automatic; can be delayed (e.g., hotels).
Card Network / SchemeThe rails that route messages between issuers and acquirers. Visa, Mastercard, Amex, Discover, etc.
Card-on-File (COF)A stored card credential used for repeat or recurring charges without re-prompting the customer.
ChargebackDispute initiated by the cardholder via their issuer. Funds are clawed back from the merchant pending investigation.
ClearingNetwork step that exchanges official transaction data between issuer and acquirer in preparation for settlement.
CVV / CVC / CIDThe 3- or 4-digit security code on the back (or front, for Amex) of a card. Proves possession of the physical card.
Decline CodeNumeric reason why an auth was refused. Common: 05 (do not honor), 51 (insufficient funds), 14 (invalid card), 54 (expired card).
EMVThe chip-card standard (Europay-Mastercard-Visa). Cryptographically prevents counterfeit cards at the POS.
EncryptionReversible scrambling of data with a key (TLS in transit, AES at rest). Useless to attackers without the key.
GatewaySoftware that captures payment data from the merchant's site and forwards it to the processor.
InterchangeFee paid by the acquirer to the issuer for each transaction. Set by the network, kept by the issuer. The largest single fee in card payments.
Issuer / Issuing BankThe cardholder's bank — issued the card and ultimately approves or declines each transaction.
ISO 8583The legacy message format used for card transactions across most networks. Slowly being replaced by ISO 20022.
KYC / AMLKnow Your Customer / Anti-Money-Laundering — regulatory checks PSPs and banks run on merchants and consumers.
MCCMerchant Category Code — 4-digit code identifying the merchant's industry (e.g., 5812 = restaurants). Drives interchange rates and rewards.
MOTOMail Order / Telephone Order — card-not-present transactions taken over the phone. Higher fraud risk, higher fees.
Network TokenA token issued by Visa/MC themselves (not the merchant or PSP). Auto-updates when the underlying card is reissued.
NFCNear-Field Communication — the tap-to-pay radio standard underlying contactless cards and Apple/Google Pay.
PANPrimary Account Number — the actual card number on the front of a card. The most sensitive piece of data in payments.
PCI DSSPayment Card Industry Data Security Standard. The security rulebook every party touching card data must follow.
ProcessorRoutes transaction messages between merchant, network, and banks. Often bundled with the acquirer.
PSPPayment Service Provider — umbrella term for an entity that combines gateway, processor, and (often) acquiring (Stripe, Adyen, PayPal).
Refund vs VoidVoid cancels an auth before settlement (free, instant). Refund reverses a settled transaction (slower, may incur fees).
SCAStrong Customer Authentication — EU/UK regulatory requirement (PSD2) mandating two-factor authentication on most online card payments. Usually satisfied via 3DS.
SettlementThe actual movement of funds from issuer to acquirer (and on to the merchant). Typically T+1 to T+3 business days.
Soft Decline / Hard DeclineSoft = retryable (insufficient funds, do not honor). Hard = don't retry (lost/stolen card, invalid card).
TLSTransport Layer Security — the modern encryption protocol securing data in transit (the "S" in HTTPS).
TokenizationReplacing the PAN with a non-sensitive surrogate ("token") that's worthless to a thief without access to the token vault.
WalletA consumer-facing layer that stores payment credentials and rides on top of underlying card or bank rails (Apple Pay, Google Pay, PayPal).
